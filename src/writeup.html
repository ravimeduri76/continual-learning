The theory behind The Forgetting Machine

Five Dials of Plasticity

Continual learning is not new. It was solved sixty years ago — for the easy case — and everything since is the same idea under harder constraints. Forty years of methods turn out to be one update rule with five knobs.

Play the game → ~10 min read

The three-beat story

Classic ML already did it. Online logistic regression, the perceptron, and above all FTRL-Proximal — which ran Google's ad-click models in production, fully online, no human in the loop (McMahan et al., KDD 2013). When the model is convex there is a single optimum, so learning as you go cannot destroy what came before. Continual learning, for convex models, is a solved and high-consequence technology. The periodic batch retraining most teams do is a choice, not a limit of the math.

Deep networks do not get it for free. Left alone, a deep net catastrophically forgets: fine-tune on new data and the old capability is overwritten, because gradient descent on a non-convex loss simply follows wherever the new data pulls. The ability to "keep part of the past and adapt fast" is something we engineer — by freezing layers, anchoring weights to the old solution, or rehearsing old data. It is bolted on, not built in.

Transformers are just big deep networks. So the same mechanisms apply — that is exactly what LoRA and fine-tuning are. The open question is not can they learn continuously; mechanically they can. It is how to do it without forgetting, at a compute cost someone will pay, with a clear owner for the update loop. Part research problem, part product problem.

One update rule

Write the update for one group of parameters k (a layer, a head, an adapter, a single weight):

θₖ ← θₖ − ηₖ · Πₖ [ ∇θₖℒ(θ ; 𝒟ₜ ∪ ℛₜ) + λₖ Mₖ (θₖ − aₖ) ]

Every continual-learning method in the last four decades is a setting of five knobs in this one line:

DialSymbolWhat it controls
Locusθₖ, Πₖwhich parameters may move, and along what projection (freeze = don't move; mask = move only some)
Paceηₖhow fast — equivalently Neff, how many past examples it still answers to
Anchoraₖwhat it is pulled back toward — the origin, the previous solution, or the pretrained init
GeometryMₖin which directions the pull acts — identity, the Fisher, or a hard mask
Evidence𝒟ₜ ∪ ℛₜwhat the gradient is allowed to see — the new stream only, or the stream plus replayed memory

Two corner cases are worth naming. Freezing is simply ηₖ = 0. A prototype / nearest-centroid classifier is the closed-form corner — no gradients at all. And in-context learning, as we will see, is a tier whose Pace is a single forward pass.

Two facts that do the work

FTRL-Proximal and EWC are the same update

The classic online learner and the famous deep-net anti-forgetting method are, algebraically, one thing. Both solve

θ ← argminθ [ ℒnew(θ) + ½ ‖θ − anchor‖²M ]

FTRL's per-coordinate stiffness σ ∝ √Σg², AdaGrad's preconditioner, the Kalman posterior precision, and EWC's diagonal Fisher Fᵢ = 𝔼[(∂log p/∂θᵢ)²] are the same statistic — the second moment of the gradient — wearing four job titles: an adaptive learning rate, a posterior precision, a synaptic importance. Google shipped elastic weight consolidation, in all but name, four years before the PNAS paper made it famous.

Locus beats Geometry

The field spent 2016–2020 on the Geometry dial (EWC, SI, MAS) and it produced the least — pure weight-space penalties sit near chance on class-incremental Split-MNIST. The field is now spending its effort on the Locus dial and it is producing the biggest numbers. Sparse-memory finetuning cuts degradation on NaturalQuestions from −89% (full fine-tune) to −71% (LoRA) to −11% — purely by changing which parameters are eligible to move. Where a trace lives beats how you regularise it.

How the dials turn in each regime

Classic ML — the convex case

Online convex optimisation is the clean version of the problem. FTRL-Proximal keeps a running penalty toward its own past iterates:

θₜ₊₁ = argminθ [ Σs≤t gₛ·θ + ½ Σs σₛ‖θ − θₛ‖² + λ₁‖θ‖₁ ] , σₜ ∝ √(Σ gₛ²)

Dial settings: Locus = every weight (the model is small); Pace shrinks as 1/√Σg²; Anchor = the previous iterate; Geometry = the diagonal second moment; Evidence = the stream alone — no replay needed, because convexity does the remembering for you. There is a single optimum, so the regret is provably sublinear and forgetting is bounded, never catastrophic.

Consequence in the wild: this is a workhorse, not a toy — FTRL ran ad auctions at planetary scale. The reason continual learning "feels unsolved" today is that we left this comfortable convex world.

Deep networks — the non-convex case

Drop convexity and the trouble begins. The loss surface has many basins; SGD on a new task moves θ into a basin that need not preserve the old input→output map. To first order, the damage to the old task is the gradient interference

Δℒold ≈ −η ⟨ ∇ℒold , ∇ℒnew ⟩

which is why forgetting collapses when the two gradients stop overlapping — and why pretraining at scale reduces forgetting for free: bigger pretrained representations are more orthogonal (Ramasesh et al., ICLR 2022). The three usable knobs:

  • Locus (Πₖ). Freeze the early layers, or gradually unfreeze, or linear-probe-then-fine-tune. LP-FT (Kumar et al., ICLR 2022) proves why the order matters: a randomly-initialised head produces a large early gradient that drags the feature extractor into directions the in-distribution data cannot constrain, distorting exactly the pretrained features you wanted to keep. Probe first, then fine-tune. This is the strongest lever.
  • Anchor + Geometry (aₖ, Mₖ). Elastic Weight Consolidation regularises toward the previous solution θ*, weighted by importance: ℒnew + ½λ Σᵢ Fᵢ(θᵢ − θ*ᵢ)². That is precisely FTRL's proximal term with M = Fisher. Synaptic Intelligence is the path-integral sibling.
  • Evidence (ℛₜ). Keep a replay buffer of old examples and mix them into each batch; the rehearsed gradient stands in for ∇ℒold so the new step cannot quietly erase it.

Consequence in the wild: transfer learning — freeze a pretrained backbone, train a fresh head — is the everyday form of this, and it is nothing more than the Locus dial turned to zero on most of the network.

Transformers — the same knobs at scale, plus a new tier

A transformer is a deep network, so all of the above still holds. Two things change at scale.

First, Locus wins decisively. Restricting which parameters may move — LoRA's low-rank adapters, or sparse-memory finetuning that edits only a handful of memory slots — is what turns catastrophic forgetting into a rounding error: the −89% → −71% → −11% ladder on NaturalQuestions is entirely a Locus effect.

Left to right the Locus dial tightens — fewer parameters may move (red) — and knowledge kept on NaturalQuestions (green) climbs from 11% to 89%. Same model, same new data; the only thing that changes is which weights are allowed to move.

Second, in-context learning is a genuinely new dial position: learning with no weight update at all. The context behaves like a rank-1 edit to the first MLP weight matrix (Dherin et al., 2025),

ΔW = W · A(C, x) · xᵀ / ‖x‖²

so its Pace is a single forward pass, its Anchor is θpretrained exactly, and its Evidence is whatever fits in the prompt. It is the fastest, most reversible dial we have — and the most transient: the trace is discarded the moment the context is evicted.

What is left is not compute alone. Yes, retraining a frontier model continuously is expensive, and someone has to own the update loop — those are real barriers, and underrated ones. But there is also a genuine unsolved technical problem underneath, which is the same one that shows up everywhere the moment you try to make learning permanent.

The invariant nobody has escaped

Every fast mechanism is transient — in-context learning is discarded at eviction, test-time training at the end of the sequence, an agent's scratchpad at the end of the episode. Every persistent mechanism is destructive — fine-tuning, model editing, even self-generated edits degrade earlier tasks as they accumulate. Nothing yet moves a trace from fast to slow without collateral damage. That is what sleep does for brains and what no deployed system does. It is the problem actually worth working on — and the reason "just fine-tune it continuously" is not the answer.

There is also a conservation law you can feel in the game: within a single tier, retention and adaptation trade against a fixed evidence budget. The only way out is to partition — run several tiers at different clocks — which is what every serious 2026 proposal, from sparse-memory finetuning to Nested Learning, is quietly doing.
